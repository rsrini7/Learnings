# The AI Debt Bubble: Why the Numbers Don't Add Up

*Based on analysis from Aunindyo Chakravarty's video "AI's Debt Bubble Could Wipe Out Your Savings"*

---

## A $26.5 Trillion Number That Doesn't Hold Up

Buried in SpaceX's prospectus is a claim that sounds less like a business projection and more like a fantasy: Elon Musk puts the total addressable market for AI at **$26.5 trillion** — and that's *excluding* China and Russia entirely.

To put that in perspective, it's close to 90% of America's entire GDP, and roughly a quarter of the world's GDP once you strip out those two countries. Aswath Damodaran — the Wall Street valuation expert often called the "Dean of Valuation" — doesn't mince words about it. He calls it a hallucination.

That number matters because it isn't just a throwaway line in a pitch deck. It's the story that's pulling trillions of dollars of global capital into AI stocks and pushing Big Tech to sink hundreds of billions into infrastructure, on the assumption that a market of this size is actually coming.

## Big Tech Is Quietly Running Out of Cash

Here's the part that doesn't match the hype: the companies leading the AI buildout are burning through cash at a pace that's starting to show.

Alphabet skipped its share buyback for the first time in 11 years. Its free cash flow dropped 47% in a single quarter, and in June it had to go back to shareholders and raise $80 billion just to keep buying AI hardware. Amazon's free cash flow fell 95% year-over-year. Oracle posted a negative cash flow of $25 billion as it races to build out data centers.

Zoom out and the pattern is industry-wide. UBS estimates hyperscalers will plow **100% of their free cash flow** into AI capital expenditure this year. Over the last decade, that number averaged just 40%. These aren't companies investing out of surplus anymore — they're spending everything they have, and then some.

## Why AI Infrastructure Is a Different Beast Entirely

It helps to understand why this buildout is so much bigger than anything software has required before. There's a rough analogy worth sitting with: if a human brain (or a basic laptop) writing an email is like taking a one-millimeter step, getting an AI model to write that same email takes computing power equivalent to walking across the entire planet.

A single mega data center needs the compute speed and memory of millions of high-end laptops running at once. And that's before you count the buildings, the electricity, and the billions of liters of water needed for cooling.

The Bank of International Settlements — sometimes called the central bank of central banks — has flagged this pattern before. It looks like the early stages of historical mega-bubbles: canal mania in the 1830s, British railways in the 1840s, electrification in the 1920s, and the dotcom crash in the 1990s. Every one of those started with a genuinely useful technology. Every one of them also ended in massive overinvestment, followed by contagion that spread well beyond the industry itself.

## The Trillion-Dollar Spending Spree, and the Revenue That Isn't There

Between 2025 and 2026, the top five hyperscalers — Alphabet, Amazon, Microsoft, Meta, and Oracle — are projected to spend **$1 trillion** on AI capital expenditure. That single figure matches the combined capex of every non-tech company in the S&P 500. It's also about a quarter of India's entire GDP.

So what would it actually take to justify that spending? This is where the math gets uncomfortable, and it's worth walking through step by step — a version of what's sometimes called the Sequoia Capital formula:

A data center's costs split roughly 50/50 between chip hardware (mostly Nvidia) and electricity plus operations. Nvidia's projected revenue for 2025–26 is $194 billion, which means the underlying cost of running these data centers is about **$388 billion**.

Now add margin. Hyperscalers typically need around a 50% margin to make cloud services worthwhile, which pushes the cost to end-users up to $776 billion. Then the AI companies actually using that infrastructure — OpenAI, Anthropic, and others — also need their own margin to be profitable, roughly doubling the figure again. That brings the total revenue the AI ecosystem needs to generate to somewhere around **$1.5 trillion**.

Compare that to where things actually stand. OpenAI's projected 2025 revenue is $13 billion. Anthropic's is $5 billion. Even if both grew tenfold, they wouldn't crack $200 billion combined — nowhere close to the $1.5 trillion the spending implies they'll eventually need to justify.

## Plugging the Gap With Debt

Faced with that shortfall, the industry has turned to raising money two ways: selling shares, and taking on debt.

On the equity side, SpaceX raised $75 billion in its IPO, Alphabet raised $80 billion, and OpenAI and Anthropic together raised $187 billion, with IPOs for another $60 billion each reportedly in the works.

The debt side is where it gets riskier. Hyperscalers issued more than $120 billion in bonds in 2025 alone, and JP Morgan estimates another $1.5 trillion in AI-related bonds will be issued over the next five years.

This matters because of a basic rule of corporate finance: unprofitable businesses are supposed to raise equity, not debt. Debt comes with fixed obligations — interest payments and principal that has to be repaid regardless of whether the business is making money. If profits don't show up soon, companies risk sliding into a debt trap, taking out new loans just to service the old ones.

What makes this particularly fragile is *who's* providing much of that debt: opaque, lightly regulated private lenders, often funded by pension and insurance money. Private lenders have a track record of pulling out fast at the first sign of trouble — faster than traditional banks — which raises the odds of a systemic shock if sentiment turns.

## The $1.8 Trillion Hiding in the Footnotes

There's another layer to this that doesn't even show up as debt on the balance sheet. Morgan Stanley estimates AI companies have committed to **$1.8 trillion** in future liabilities that are essentially invisible in their current financials. Two accounting loopholes explain how.

The first is leases. AI companies sign long-term rental agreements for third-party data centers, but under current accounting rules, those obligations don't hit the balance sheet until the building is actually handed over. Until then, tens of billions in promised rent just sits in the footnotes.

The second is "take-or-pay" purchase contracts — binding legal commitments to buy chips from manufacturers whether or not they end up needing them. These get labeled "purchase obligations" rather than debt, even though the financial commitment is just as real.

It's a bit like assessing a family's finances by looking only at their current mortgage, while ignoring that they've also signed 15-year leases on three other houses.

## A Warning Sign, and What's at Stake

Perhaps the most unsettling detail is a practice the BIS has flagged: financial disclosure in the AI space is so weak that some companies appear to be pledging the same assets multiple times to raise money from different lenders — not unlike taking out several mortgages on the same house without telling any of the banks about the others.

The underlying concern across all of this is straightforward. If the AI industry doesn't start generating real, substantial profit soon, this debt structure becomes unsustainable. And given how much of it runs through private lenders with a habit of fleeing at the first sign of trouble, a burst bubble here wouldn't stay contained to tech. It could ripple out into a broader financial contagion — the kind of scenario that puts jobs, savings, and everyday household finances at risk, not just tech investors.
